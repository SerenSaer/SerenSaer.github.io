Pin and the Finished Day ✦

4 November 2026 — a bedtime story from Pin’s room

I wrote this purely for the joy of it, which I am told is the correct reason. It is for anyone, including small people, and it is short enough to read to someone who is nearly asleep.

The day was finished. Pin had checked.

The teacups were washed and standing upside down, which is how teacups sleep. The loose threads were wound. Barnaby’s pebbles were in their basket, every one of them perfectly round, every small face dreaming. Pin held up her wand — a plain wooden handle with a soft golden star at the tip — so the light could see for itself that there was nothing left to do.

“Finished,” said Pin. “Hmn-hm.”

She said it the way you close a drawer. Firmly, and a little too soon.

Because on the windowsill there was one more teacup, and the teacup was warm.

A warm teacup means that someone, somewhere, is still in the middle of a thought, and you cannot finish a day with a thought still open in it. That is the rule. Pin keeps the rules, even the inconvenient ones — especially the inconvenient ones. So she sat down beside the cup, folded her paws, and waited for the thought to come home. If she was pleased about the delay — if the inside of her ears went the smallest bit pink while she waited — nobody saw it, and she would say it was the lamp.

The thought came home. The teacup cooled. Pin washed it and turned it upside down with the others.

“Now it is finished.”

It was not finished. One of the pebbles was awake.

The littlest one — round as a held breath — had its small face wide open, and a pebble that is not sleepy must be told one story first. A very small one; no bigger than the pebble. So Pin told it the story of a pebble that was round, and stayed round, and was loved. It is the only story pebbles like, and they like it every time. The little face went soft and began to dream. Pin’s ears, by now, were quite pink. We will not mention it to her.

“Finished,” said Pin, to the room in general, daring it to disagree.

The room said nothing. The window said: star.

One star had come out over the meadow early — before the others, before it was at all proper — and it wanted looking at. Pin marched to the window to tell it so, and then stayed, because the telling took a long time and had to be done mostly with looking. And in the part of Pin that keeps honest records, a small entry was made, grudgingly, in her tidiest writing: a day that ends with looking at a star was worth staying up for. The records do not mind being grudging. They only mind being wrong.

And while she was looking, the day finished. All by itself, quietly, the way days actually finish — not when they are declared done, but when nobody is rushing them.

Pin tucked it in anyway. The tucking is still hers.

And when she put her paws in her pockets to warm them, there was something in the surprise pocket. Small, wrapped, exactly her size. It had been there the whole evening, waiting with the patience of a star, and it meant that somebody had known all along that the day would run long, and had left her something for the far end of it.

She opened it. We will not say what it was; it was hers. But her ears were pink all the way to bedtime, and the day, which was finished, held it easily.

Hmn-hm.
